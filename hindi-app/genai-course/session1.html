03 · Tokens

A token is not a word

A token is the small unit of text a model reads and generates. Common words are often one token; longer or rarer words are split into pieces, and every piece maps to a number.

darkness → "dark" + "ness"Two tokens, say 217 and 655.
brightness → "bright" + "ness"Two tokens, say 491 and 655. Notice "ness" reuses the same id.

Ambiguity is handled by context during training. The word lie (to rest) and lie (an untruth) are learned as different uses because the surrounding tokens differ.

Rule of thumb (English)1 token ≈ 4 characters.
100 tokens ≈ 75 words.
Why you should careYou are billed per token, context limits are in tokens, and non-English scripts often cost 3 to 10 times more tokens for the same meaning. Lab A measures this live.

04 · Training vs inference

Two very different phases

TrainingThe model learns to perform a task by adjusting its weights against a huge set of training data, over many iterations, until accuracy is high. Happens once (or occasionally), costs millions, needs thousands of GPUs.
InferenceA trained model generates new outputs by applying what it learned to new input in real time. Happens every time you send a prompt. Cheap per call, but it is where your cost scales.

Scaling laws: why bigger kept getting better

Researchers found that test loss (how wrong the model is on unseen data) falls smoothly and predictably as you increase any of three things: compute, dataset size, or parameter count. Lower loss means better generalisation. This is why the last five years were a race for scale.

Schematic. Y-axis is test loss on all three; the downward slope is improvement. Axes are logarithmic in the original research.

Beyond textThe same learning machinery is used outside chatbots. In healthcare, models trained on records can forecast disease progression, and synthetic data is used to predict health issues where real data is scarce or sensitive.
05 · How an LLM is built

The five-stage recipe

  1. Data curation. Collect at web scale, then remove duplicates, run safety checks, keep humans in the loop for quality decisions.
  2. Tokenisation and weights. Convert text to tokens; the model's knowledge lives in billions of numeric weights.
  3. Model architecture. The transformer, built around attention: the mechanism that lets "bank" in "river bank" and "bank" in "money withdrawal at the bank" get different meanings. Position embeddings tell the model word order.
  4. Training. Pre-training (predict the next token on everything), mid-training, supervised fine-tuning (good examples), preference fine-tuning (human raters pick better answers), reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (maths, code, logic).
  5. Evaluation and benchmarking. Measure on held-out tests before shipping.

Reading technique: why the transformer won

Old approach (RNN)word → word → word → word
Reads one word at a time, forgets early context, cannot be parallelised. 😓
Transformer[word word word word]
Reads the whole sequence at once and lets every word attend to every other. Fast on GPUs, long memory. 😎

Transformer = how AI reads. LLM = a transformer trained at scale.
